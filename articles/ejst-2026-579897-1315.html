Global Neurocognitive Vulnerability in the Anthropocene: A Narrative Review of Physiological-Psychological Impacts of Extreme Heat and Evidence-Based Policy Interventions

Nirosha Rathnakumari

Archive home / Issue 4

Pages: 331-341   DOI: 10.48309/ejst.2026.579897.1315

Abstract

The Anthropocene is characterized by an unprecedented rise in extreme heat events, posing a systemic threat to global neurocognitive health. While physiological impacts are well documented, neurocognitive vulnerabilities, ranging from acute cognitive inefficiency to heatstroke-related neuroinflammation and psychiatric morbidity, remain inadequately integrated into public health policy. The purpose of this narrative review is to synthesize multidisciplinary evidence on the physiological and psychological pathways through which extreme heat may affect cognition and mental health, and to consider the implications for heat-health policy and nature-based interventions. The review draws on targeted searches of PubMed and Google Scholar and integrates evidence across evolutionary perspectives, molecular biology, pediatrics, psychiatry, occupational health, and urban planning. Evidence indicates that acute heat exposure can impair aspects of attention, executive function, and cognitive control, although the magnitude and direction of effects vary by task, exposure intensity, acclimatization, and individual strain. Severe heat illness can involve blood-brain barrier disruption and neuroinflammatory responses, while nocturnal heat can disturb thermoregulation and sleep. Emerging human evidence also links prenatal heat exposure with neurodevelopmental outcomes, but this literature remains limited and should be interpreted cautiously. Current heat- health planning gives greater attention to physical morbidity than to mental health and neurocognitive risks. Nature-Based Solutions (NbS) may contribute to heat mitigation, although direct neurocognitive benefits require further evaluation.
